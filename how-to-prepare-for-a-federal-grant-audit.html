How to prepare your organization for a federal grant audit

A federal grant audit is a test of whether your records can prove what your reports claim. For most recipients that means the Single Audit under 2 CFR 200 Subpart F, which examines your financial statements and your compliance with the requirements that apply to your major federal programs. Preparation is not a spring project. It is a set of routines, kept all year, that produce the documents the auditor will ask for before anyone asks.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • The auditor tests compliance requirements listed in the annual Compliance Supplement, program by program. Know which requirements apply to yours.
  • Your Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards is the starting point for the whole audit. Reconcile it to the ledger before you hand it over.
  • Prior findings are retested. An open corrective action plan is the first thing the auditor reads.
  • The records that matter most are the ones that show a decision was made before the money moved: allowability, procurement method, effort, subrecipient risk.
  • Audit readiness and good administration are the same work. Organizations that are ready for the audit are the ones that ran the award well.

Which audit are you preparing for?

"Federal grant audit" covers several things. The most common is the Single Audit, required when an organization expends federal awards above the threshold in 2 CFR 200.501 in a fiscal year. It is performed by your independent auditor and reported to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. Separately, an awarding agency may conduct its own monitoring review or site visit, an Office of Inspector General may audit a specific award, and a pass-through entity may review you as its subrecipient. The documents they ask for overlap almost entirely, so preparing for the Single Audit prepares you for the rest.

What the Single Audit examines

Subpart F directs the auditor to two things. First, your financial statements, audited under generally accepted government auditing standards. Second, your compliance with the federal requirements that apply to each major program, as identified through the risk-based approach in sections 200.518 through 200.520. For each major program, the auditor works from the compliance requirements in the OMB Compliance Supplement for that year. The requirements that most often matter for nonprofits and governments include:

  • Activities allowed or unallowed and allowable costs. Were funds spent on what the program permits, and do the charges pass the cost principles in Subpart E?
  • Cash management. Were draws limited to immediate need, and was interest on advances handled correctly?
  • Eligibility. Were participants, subrecipients, or beneficiaries eligible, and is that documented?
  • Equipment and real property management. Inventory records, physical counts, disposition.
  • Matching, level of effort, earmarking. Was cost share real, allowable, and documented?
  • Period of performance. Were costs incurred inside the dates on the award?
  • Procurement and suspension and debarment. Method documented, competition where required, SAM exclusion checks before awarding contracts.
  • Program income. Accounted for and applied as the award requires.
  • Reporting. Financial and performance reports filed on time and reconcilable to the books.
  • Subrecipient monitoring. Risk assessments, required subaward terms, monitoring evidence, audit follow-up.
  • Special tests and provisions. Program-specific requirements such as Davis-Bacon wage compliance or environmental review.

Your responsibilities as the auditee

Section 200.508 lists what the organization being audited must do: procure the audit, prepare the financial statements and the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards (SEFA), promptly follow up on findings with a corrective action plan, and give the auditor access to records and people. Section 200.511 requires a summary schedule of prior audit findings and a corrective action plan for current ones. Section 200.512 sets the deadline for submitting the reporting package to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. Tribal governments have a specific option under that section regarding public posting of their audit, worth reading if it applies.

The records trail, built before anyone asks

Auditors ask for documents that show a decision was made correctly at the time. The organizations that struggle are not usually the ones that spent money badly; they are the ones that cannot prove they spent it well. Build these files as you go:

  1. The award file. Notice of award, all modifications, approved budget, terms and conditions, agency correspondence, prior-approval requests and responses.
  2. The transaction file. For each charge: invoice or receipt, approval, allocation basis if shared, proof of payment, and the ledger entry that ties it to the award.
  3. Personnel support. Records that reflect actual effort on each award and support the salary distribution, reviewed by someone with knowledge of the work.
  4. Procurement files. For each purchase: the method, why that method, the solicitation or quotes, the selection rationale, the SAM exclusion check, and the executed contract with the Appendix II provisions.
  5. Subrecipient files. The written subrecipient-versus-contractor determination, the risk assessment, the subaward with the required data elements, monitoring reports, their Single Audit results, and your follow-up.
  6. Reporting files. Every SF-425 and performance report, with the working papers that reconcile them to the general ledger.
  7. Policies. Written financial management, procurement, allowability, cash management, conflict-of-interest, travel, and record-retention policies, dated, approved, and matching actual practice.

A readiness routine for the ninety days before fieldwork

  • Reconcile the SEFA. Every award, with the correct Assistance Listing number, pass-through identifiers, amounts expended, and amounts passed to subrecipients, tied to the ledger.
  • Re-read last year's findings. Confirm each corrective action was taken, and that you can show it.
  • Sample yourself. Pull twenty transactions across awards and walk each one through the records trail above. Fix what breaks.
  • Confirm the calendar. Every report that was due during the year, with proof it was filed on time.
  • Update the policies. If practice has drifted from the written policy, change one or the other now.
  • Brief the people. Program staff who will be interviewed should understand what the auditor is testing and where the documents live.

When a finding comes anyway

A finding is not a catastrophe. It becomes one when it is repeated. Write a corrective action plan that names the person responsible, the specific change, and the date by which it is complete. Then do it, and keep the evidence. Pass-through entities and agencies look at whether findings recur far more closely than whether they occurred.

FAQ

Common questions

How do I know whether we need a Single Audit this year?+
Total the federal awards you expended during your fiscal year, including federal money received as a subrecipient, and compare it to the current threshold in 2 CFR 200.501. The threshold changed in the 2024 revision, so confirm it in the regulation rather than relying on a remembered figure.
Can the auditor look at any award, or only the big ones?+
The auditor identifies major programs using the risk-based approach in Subpart F. Large programs are usually major, but a smaller program can be selected because of risk, prior findings, or agency request. Every award should be ready.
What is the single most useful preparation step?+
Reconcile the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards to the general ledger before fieldwork begins. Nearly every audit starts there, and an SEFA that does not tie sets the tone for everything that follows.
