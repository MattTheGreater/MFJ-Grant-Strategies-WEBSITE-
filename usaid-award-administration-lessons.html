USAID award administration: lessons for federal grant recipients

Administering awards from the funder's side reveals what recipients rarely see: the award is managed from a file, and the file is what the agency believes about you. Agency grants officers and agreement officer's representatives work from the award record, the reports you submit, the approvals you request, and the audits on file. A recipient that keeps that record complete, asks before acting, and reports on time is treated as low risk. One that does not is treated as a problem, whatever the quality of its program. The lessons from the funder's chair are about managing that record.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • The agency manages your award from a file. Make sure what is in it is accurate and complete.
  • Cooperative agreements carry substantial involvement. Know what the agency has reserved the right to approve.
  • Prior approval exists to protect you. A documented approval is a disallowance that cannot happen.
  • Agency staff are measured on their portfolio's compliance. Help them, and they help you.
  • Monitoring is not adversarial. It is the agency verifying that what the file says is true.

A note on perspective

The founder of MFJ Grant Strategies began his federal career in the grants unit of the U.S. Agency for International Development, where he obligated roughly $305 million across approximately 320 assistance awards. That is a funder-side figure: money the agency awarded to implementing organizations, administered from the agency's chair. The lessons here are drawn from that vantage point and apply well beyond USAID, because the mechanics of federal assistance are the same across agencies even where the regulations differ. USAID assistance is governed by 2 CFR 200 as supplemented by 2 CFR 700 and the agency's Automated Directives System, chapter 303 among others. A domestic recipient under HHS, HUD, or DOJ works under the same Uniform Guidance with a different agency supplement.

Lesson one: the award is managed from a file

Inside an agency, an award is a record: the agreement and its modifications, the approved budget, the implementation plan, the reports received, the approvals requested and granted, the audit findings, and the correspondence. The grants officer and the agreement officer's representative (AOR), or the program officer in domestic agencies, make decisions from that record. When a recipient asks for a no-cost extension, a budget realignment, or a change in key personnel, the officer looks at the file to decide whether the recipient has earned the benefit of the doubt.

The practical lesson is that the recipient is building that file with every submission. A complete, accurate, on-time record is the strongest asset an organization has with its funder, and a thin or inconsistent one is a liability that no amount of program quality offsets.

Lesson two: cooperative agreements are not grants

USAID uses cooperative agreements extensively, and so do many domestic agencies. The difference from a grant is substantial involvement: the agency reserves specific rights, typically approval of the implementation plan and key personnel, involvement in the selection of subrecipients, and approval of monitoring and evaluation plans. These are written into the agreement. A recipient that treats a cooperative agreement like a grant and acts without the reserved approvals has violated the award even when the action was sensible. Read the substantial involvement clause before the first month, and build each reserved approval into the project calendar.

Lesson three: prior approval protects the recipient

From the recipient's side, prior approval feels like friction. From the funder's side, it is the mechanism that makes a cost defensible. A documented agency approval of a budget shift, a change in scope, equipment purchase, or foreign travel settles the allowability question permanently. The agency cannot later disallow what it approved in writing. Recipients that avoid asking, to avoid delay, trade a few weeks of waiting for a permanent exposure. Ask early, in writing, with the justification the officer needs to say yes, and keep the response in the award file.

Lesson four: agency staff are on your side, within limits

Grants officers and AORs are evaluated on their portfolios. Late reports, unresolved findings, and unliquidated balances reflect on them. A recipient that files on time, flags problems before they become findings, and responds to requests promptly is making the officer's job easier, and officers remember. That relationship is worth more than any single award. It is the difference between an extension granted and one denied, between a realignment approved in days and one that sits for months.

Lesson five: monitoring is verification, not suspicion

Site visits, desk reviews, and financial reviews exist because the agency is required to verify that the file reflects reality. A recipient with working systems finds monitoring uneventful: the records are where they should be, the staff can explain them, and the numbers tie. The recipients that experience monitoring as adversarial are usually the ones for whom the file and the reality have diverged. The best preparation for monitoring is the same as the best preparation for an audit: administer the award as if someone will check, because someone will.

Lesson six: closeout is where unfinished business surfaces

From the funder's side, closeout is when every unresolved item becomes visible at once: unliquidated obligations, unreconciled advances, missing final reports, equipment without disposition instructions, and subrecipient audits never reviewed. Agencies close awards on their own terms when recipients miss the closeout window, and the recipient inherits whatever the file shows. Start closeout planning before the period of performance ends, reconcile cash to the penny, and submit the final reports inside the deadline in 2 CFR 200.344 and the award terms.

What this means for a domestic recipient

Whether the funder is USAID, HHS, HUD, DOJ, or a state passing federal money through, the recipient's position is the same. It is building a record that the funder will use to judge it. Keep the record complete. Know what the agreement reserves to the agency. Ask before acting. File on time. Treat agency staff as colleagues with their own obligations. And close out as carefully as you applied.

FAQ

Common questions

How is USAID assistance different from domestic federal grants?+
The core rules are the same: 2 CFR 200 applies, supplemented by 2 CFR 700 and USAID policy. The differences are in the supplement, the heavier use of cooperative agreements with substantial involvement, international cost and procurement considerations, and USAID-specific standard provisions. The administrative discipline required is identical.
What does an agreement officer's representative actually do?+
The AOR is the agency's technical and programmatic point of contact for the award, responsible for monitoring performance, reviewing reports, and recommending approvals to the agreement officer, who holds the legal authority to modify the award. Keeping the AOR informed is the recipient's most important relationship.
Does the founder's USAID figure mean MFJ has won USAID funding for clients?+
No. The roughly $305 million figure is money the agency obligated across awards during his tenure in its grants unit, administered from the funder's side. It is not funding awarded to him or raised by MFJ, which was founded in 2025. The founder page states exactly what each figure means.
