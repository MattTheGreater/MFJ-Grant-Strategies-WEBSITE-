What federal grant reviewers look for in competitive applications

Federal grant reviewers score applications against the published criteria in the notice of funding opportunity, under time pressure, with a scoring sheet in front of them. They are looking for the answer to each criterion, in the order the criteria appear, stated plainly enough to find and specific enough to score. An application that makes that job easy, and that gives evidence rather than assertion for every point, is the one that scores well. Elegance matters far less than findability and proof.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • The rubric is published. Reviewers are not guessing what matters, and neither should you.
  • Reviewers read many applications quickly. Headings that mirror the criteria and specific claims near the top of each section earn points.
  • Assertions without evidence score as zero. Local data, named partners, and measurable outcomes are what evidence looks like.
  • The budget is read as proof of the plan. Mismatches are among the most common reasons scores drop.
  • Panels are human. Clarity, honesty about limitations, and a realistic timeline read as competence.

How a review panel works

Most federal competitive programs use an objective review: a panel of reviewers, often three per application, who score independently against published criteria and then reconcile their scores in discussion. Reviewers are typically subject-matter practitioners, sometimes agency staff, sometimes outside experts recruited for the cycle. They receive a stack of applications, a scoring rubric drawn directly from the NOFO, and a deadline. The founder of MFJ has sat on these panels as an independent federal reviewer, and the lessons below are the ones that hold across programs.

What reviewers are actually doing

A reviewer is trying to answer, for each criterion, "how well does this application address it, and what is the evidence?" They are writing comments that justify a score. The easier you make it to find the answer and the evidence, the higher the score, because the reviewer can write a confident justification. Applications lose points not because the reviewer disagreed, but because the reviewer could not find the answer, or found a claim with nothing behind it.

Seven things that earn points

  1. Headings that mirror the criteria. If the NOFO's criteria are Need, Approach, Capacity, and Evaluation, those are your headings, in that order. Reviewers should never have to hunt.
  2. The answer first, then the support. Open each section with the direct response to the criterion, then build the case. A reviewer skimming the first sentence of each section should be able to score the application.
  3. Evidence that is local and specific. National statistics establish that a problem exists somewhere. Local data, a needs assessment, a community survey, a named partner's commitment letter, establish that it exists here and that you are positioned to address it.
  4. Outcomes that can be measured inside the period of performance. "Improve community health" cannot be scored. "Increase the share of enrolled patients with a documented care plan from 40 to 70 percent by month 24, measured in the practice's EHR" can.
  5. A plan with a calendar. Who does what, in which quarter, with what milestone. Reviewers test the approach by asking whether it could actually happen as written.
  6. Capacity shown, not claimed. Prior awards administered, audit results, staff named with relevant experience, written policies in place, and a realistic staffing plan. Reviewers notice when every position is "to be hired."
  7. A budget that tells the same story. Every line traceable to an activity in the narrative, arithmetic that foots, an indirect rate with a basis, and cost share with a named source.

Seven things that lose points

  • Reorganizing the criteria into the applicant's preferred story, so the reviewer must reassemble it.
  • Unsupported superlatives. "Innovative," "unique," "proven," and "robust" without evidence read as filler.
  • Ignoring a criterion because it seems minor. Every point in the rubric is a point.
  • Boilerplate that could have been submitted to any program by any applicant.
  • A need section that never names the applicant's own community or its data.
  • Evaluation plans with no data source, no baseline, and no one responsible.
  • Budget mismatches: staff who appear in one document and not the other, travel without activity, equipment without justification.

Write for the tired reviewer

By the twelfth application, a reviewer is tired. Short paragraphs, plain words, tables where tables help, and white space are not stylistic preferences; they are the difference between a reviewer who finds your evidence and one who does not. Avoid acronyms the reviewer may not know. Say what the limitation is and how you will handle it rather than hoping it goes unnoticed. Honesty about a risk, paired with a mitigation, reads as competence. Silence about an obvious risk reads as either inexperience or concealment.

After the score

Whether or not you are funded, request the reviewer comments. They are the most valuable feedback an organization can get on its competitive position, and they are specific to this program and this cycle. Read them against the rubric, fix what they name, and apply again. Agencies notice applicants who respond to feedback.

FAQ

Common questions

Do reviewers read every page?+
They try, but they score by criterion and they are reading quickly. Material that is not where the rubric says it should be is often missed. Put every answer where the criterion is addressed, even if it means repeating a fact.
Does writing quality matter?+
Clarity matters a great deal. Elegance matters very little. A plainly written application with specific evidence beats a beautifully written one with none.
How much does the budget affect the score?+
Often more than applicants expect. Many rubrics score budget reasonableness directly, and even where they do not, reviewers treat the budget as evidence that the plan is real. A mismatch between budget and narrative undermines every other section.
