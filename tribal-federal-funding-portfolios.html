How tribal governments can build sustainable federal funding portfolios

A sustainable tribal funding portfolio is one the nation controls, rather than a collection of awards that each control a program. That means treating Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (ISDEAA, Public Law 93-638) contracts and compacts, formula funding, and competitive grants as one system, administered under one set of controls, with the nation's own priorities deciding what gets pursued. The goal is funding that strengthens institutional capacity and self-governance, not a dependence on the next competitive cycle.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • Start from the nation's priorities, then map funding to them. A portfolio built the other way around serves the funders.
  • 638 contracts and compacts are the foundation. They carry rights and flexibilities that competitive grants do not.
  • One grants office, one calendar, one set of policies across every agency's money. Agency-by-agency administration is where tribes lose capacity.
  • Indirect cost recovery is sovereignty in practice. A current negotiated rate, or the de minimis election, pays for the capacity to manage everything else.
  • Competitive awards should build something permanent: a facility, a trained workforce, a data system, a service the nation can sustain.

Why tribal portfolios are different

A tribal nation is a sovereign government with a government-to-government relationship with the United States and, for many programs, a trust relationship as well. That changes the funding landscape in ways that no other recipient type shares. The nation can contract or compact to run federal programs itself under ISDEAA. It receives formula and base funding through the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Indian Health Service. It is eligible, as a government, for programs at HUD, DOT, DOJ, EPA, USDA, HHS, and others that are open to states and localities, often with tribal set-asides. And it carries obligations to its citizens that are permanent, while nearly all of the funding is periodic.

The consequence is that a tribal government typically holds more distinct funding streams, from more agencies, under more sets of rules, than a municipality of similar size. Without a deliberate portfolio approach, each stream grows its own records, its own reporting habits, and its own staff, and the nation's capacity fragments.

The foundation: self-determination contracts and compacts

ISDEAA allows a tribe to take over the operation of programs that BIA or IHS would otherwise run, through a Title I contract or, for tribes in the self-governance program, a Title IV or Title V compact and funding agreement. The funding follows the program, including an amount for contract support costs. A nation operating under 638 has rights that grantees do not: the ability to redesign programs within the agreement, to carry over funds, to invest unexpended balances under the terms of the Act, and to negotiate rather than apply.

These agreements are where a sustainable portfolio starts, because they are recurring, negotiated, and under the nation's control. The administrative work is not lighter, but it is stable, and the capacity built to run them well is the same capacity that administers everything else.

The second layer: formula and base funding

Beyond 638, tribes receive recurring formula allocations such as the Indian Housing Block Grant under NAHASDA, Tribal Transportation Program funds, and various HHS and USDA formula programs. These carry program-specific rules and reporting, and some, like NAHASDA, have their own regulations alongside 2 CFR 200. They are predictable, and they should be budgeted as the nation's base, with competitive funding layered above them rather than substituting for them.

The third layer: competitive awards that build something permanent

Competitive grants are where the most time is spent and the most risk sits. The discipline that keeps a tribal portfolio sustainable is simple to state: pursue competitive funding that leaves something permanent behind. A clinic expansion, a trained environmental staff, a broadband network, a court system, a data platform. Competitive awards that only fund ongoing service delivery create a cliff when they end, and the nation inherits the obligation without the money. Each pursuit should answer, in writing, what the nation will own when the award closes and how it will be sustained.

One grants office across every agency's money

The nations that administer funding well do not run it department by department. They have a central grants function, however small, that holds one calendar of every report due to every agency, one set of written policies for procurement, allowability, cash management, and subrecipient monitoring that apply regardless of the funding source, one records system, and one relationship with the auditor. Program departments own the work; the grants office owns the compliance and the reporting. That structure means a staff change in one program does not take the institutional knowledge of its federal award with it.

It also means the nation can see its whole portfolio at once: which awards end when, which obligations are unfunded, where cost share is pledged, and where the next cycle must be planned. That visibility is what turns funding from a series of reactions into a strategy.

Indirect costs and contract support

Few things advance tribal self-governance as concretely as full recovery of administrative costs. A current indirect cost rate negotiated with the nation's cognizant agency, usually the Department of the Interior's Interior Business Center, applies across federal awards and pays for the finance, grants, HR, and IT capacity that makes everything else possible. A nation without a negotiated rate may elect the de minimis rate stated in the current 2 CFR 200.414. Under ISDEAA, contract support costs are a separate entitlement that funds the reasonable costs of running contracted programs. Leaving either on the table means subsidizing federal programs with the nation's own revenue.

Compliance considerations specific to tribes

  • Sovereignty and the audit. 2 CFR 200.512 allows a tribal government to opt out of having its Single Audit reporting package made publicly available on the Federal Audit Clearinghouse, while still submitting it. Many nations exercise this; the decision should be deliberate.
  • Tribal law and federal procurement standards. Tribal procurement ordinances, including Indian preference under applicable law, must be reconciled with 2 CFR 200.317 through 200.327. Written policies should show how.
  • Tribal council action. Many awards require a council resolution. Build the lead time into every application calendar.
  • Data sovereignty. Reporting requirements involve data about tribal citizens. Decide, in policy, what is shared and how.

A portfolio review, once a year

Once a year, step back from the individual awards and look at the whole. Which agencies fund the nation, and how much of the total comes from each? Which awards end in the next eighteen months, and what happens to the programs they fund? Where is cost share pledged? Is the indirect rate current? What did the last audit find, and is it fixed? And which of the nation's priorities have no funding strategy at all? The answers are the portfolio plan.

FAQ

Common questions

Should a tribe prioritize 638 contracting over competitive grants?+
The two are not substitutes. 638 agreements are the stable foundation and deserve first attention because they are recurring and negotiated. Competitive grants build capacity on top of that foundation and should be pursued selectively, for things the nation will own when the award ends.
Do 2 CFR 200 requirements apply to 638 contracts?+
Partly. ISDEAA and its implementing regulations govern self-determination contracts and compacts, and certain Uniform Guidance provisions apply differently or not at all. Each agreement states which rules apply. Treat the question as one to answer in writing for each agreement rather than assume.
How small a tribe can justify a grants office?+
Any nation administering several federal awards benefits from one person, or one fractional arrangement, that owns the compliance calendar and the policies across all of them. The office does not need to be large. It needs to be central.
