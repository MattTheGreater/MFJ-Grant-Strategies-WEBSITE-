How to establish an effective grants management department

An effective grants management department is a central function that owns the funding pipeline and the post-award obligations across every award the organization holds. It does not need to be large. It needs clear ownership, written policies that match practice, one calendar of every deadline, a records system that survives staff turnover, and a working relationship with finance, program staff, and leadership. The size follows the portfolio; the structure is the same whether it is one person, a team, or a fractional arrangement.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • Centralize ownership. Grants administered department by department lose institutional memory with every departure.
  • Write the policies the regulation names, and write them to describe what you actually do.
  • Finance and grants are different functions that must reconcile monthly. Decide who does what in writing.
  • Build the calendar before anything else. Every report, every renewal, every closeout, in one place.
  • Size the function to the portfolio. Many organizations need a fraction of a grants office, not a whole one.

What a grants department is for

A grants office exists to do two things the rest of the organization is not built to do: decide which funding to pursue and how, and make sure every award is administered to its terms from setup through closeout. Program staff deliver the work. Finance records the money. The grants function sits between them, owning the obligations to the funder that neither of the others is positioned to see whole.

Organizations that lack this function do not lack grants. They lack a place where the grants are known, tracked, and defended. The symptoms are familiar: a report deadline discovered the week it is due, an audit finding about a subrecipient no one was monitoring, a budget shifted without approval, and a closeout that never happened.

The functions, whoever performs them

  • Strategy and pipeline. Matching organizational priorities to funders, maintaining a prospect list with realistic assessments, and deciding what not to pursue.
  • Proposal development. Managing the application calendar, writing or coordinating narrative and budget, and securing approvals and partner commitments.
  • Award setup. Reading the award terms, building the budget in the accounting system, establishing the reporting calendar, and briefing program and finance staff on what the award requires.
  • Compliance. Allowability decisions, procurement review, prior-approval requests, subrecipient determinations and monitoring, and policy maintenance.
  • Reporting. Financial reports reconciled to the ledger, performance reports tied to the approved objectives, both filed on time.
  • Records. An award file for every award, in a system that outlives any individual.
  • Audit and closeout. SEFA preparation, audit support, corrective action, and closeout on the regulation's timeline.

The policies you must have in writing

2 CFR 200 requires written policies in several areas, and auditors test both their existence and whether practice matches them. At minimum: financial management and internal control procedures; cost allowability and allocation, including how shared costs are split; procurement, including the methods used at each threshold and a conflict-of-interest standard; cash management and drawdown; personnel compensation and effort documentation; travel; property and equipment management; subrecipient monitoring; and record retention. Each should be dated, approved by the governing body or leadership, and reviewed when the regulation changes, as it did in 2024.

Systems that do not depend on a person

The most common failure in small grants offices is that the system lives in one person's head and inbox. Build instead a shared award register with every active award, its agency, its period of performance, its budget, and its reporting dates; a shared calendar that drives reminders to the people who owe each report; a document structure where every award has the same folders; and a monthly reconciliation routine between the grants register and the general ledger. None of this requires expensive software. It requires that it be written down and used.

Finance and grants: drawing the line

Grants and finance overlap, and the overlap is where things fall. Decide, in writing, who approves allowability before a purchase, who prepares the SF-425 and who reviews it, who maintains the indirect cost rate and applies it, who records cost share, and who reconciles grant ledgers to the general ledger each month. The answer can be the same person wearing two hats in a small organization, but the two hats should be named.

Sizing the function

A rough rule: the administrative effort scales with the number of awards and the number of agencies more than with dollar value. Five awards from five agencies are far more work than one award of the same total. An organization with a handful of awards usually needs part of a person, not a whole one. An organization with a dozen awards across several agencies, with subrecipients, needs a dedicated person and clear finance support. Universities and tribal governments with large portfolios need a team with pre-award and post-award specialization.

When a fractional model fits

Many organizations sit in the gap: too many awards to administer informally, too few to justify a full-time grants manager with salary, benefits, and overhead. A fractional grants department fills that gap with an outside specialist on a monthly retainer who performs the functions above, builds the policies and systems, and trains the organization's staff on them. The arrangement works best when the goal is capacity the organization keeps, and when ongoing administration can be supported through the awards' indirect cost recovery. It works poorly when the organization wants to outsource responsibility rather than build it.

Whichever model fits, the test is the same: can the organization name the person who owns every award's obligations, show the policy that governs each decision, and produce the record that proves it? If yes, it has a grants department, whatever it is called.

FAQ

Common questions

Should the grants office report to finance or to programs?+
Either can work, but it should report to someone with authority over both, because the grants function regularly has to say no to a program expenditure and yes to a finance process that programs find slow. Many organizations place it under the executive director or chief operating officer for that reason.
Do we need grants management software?+
Not to start. A shared award register, a shared calendar, and a consistent folder structure cover most organizations with fewer than a dozen awards. Software becomes worth its cost when the portfolio is large enough that manual tracking fails, and when the organization already has the routines the software would automate.
How long does it take to set up a grants function?+
A workable version, with the register, calendar, core policies, and award files in place, takes most organizations about ninety days of focused effort. Maturing it, so that routines run without reminders, takes a year of cycles.
