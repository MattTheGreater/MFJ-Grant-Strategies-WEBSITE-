The complete guide to 2 CFR 200 for nonprofit organizations

2 CFR 200, the Uniform Guidance, is the single federal rulebook that governs how a nonprofit may spend, document, and report federal grant money. It sets the administrative requirements, the cost principles that decide whether a cost is allowable, and the audit requirements that apply once federal spending passes the current Single Audit threshold. Nearly every federal award to a nonprofit incorporates it by reference, so the award letter is where you find the rule, and 2 CFR 200 is where you find out what the rule means.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • The Uniform Guidance applies to you through your award terms. Read the award, then read the subparts the award points to.
  • Subpart D (administrative requirements) and Subpart E (cost principles) do most of the work day to day. Subpart F governs the audit.
  • A cost must be allowable, allocable, reasonable, consistently treated, and documented. Miss any one and the cost is at risk.
  • Written policies are a requirement, not a nicety. Procurement, allowability, cash management, and conflict of interest all need one.
  • Thresholds change. Confirm the current figure in the regulation itself before relying on any number you remember.

What 2 CFR 200 is, and why it applies to you

Title 2 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 200, is formally titled "Uniform Administrative Requirements, Cost Principles, and Audit Requirements for Federal Awards." Grants professionals call it the Uniform Guidance. Before it took effect, nonprofits worked under a patchwork of OMB circulars, A-110 for administrative rules and A-122 for cost principles among them. The Uniform Guidance consolidated those into one part of the CFR, and most federal agencies adopted it with their own supplements in Title 2.

It applies to a nonprofit because the award says so. Federal grant and cooperative agreement terms incorporate 2 CFR 200 and the awarding agency's adopting regulation by reference. That makes the Uniform Guidance a condition of the money, and a failure to follow it a potential disallowed cost, a finding in your audit, or, in serious cases, a reason for the agency to withhold payments or terminate the award.

The regulation was substantially revised in 2024, and several dollar thresholds moved. This guide describes the structure and the obligations. For any figure, check the current text at eCFR rather than relying on a number from a training slide or an older award.

How the regulation is organized

The Uniform Guidance has six subparts and several appendices. Knowing which one you are in tells you what kind of question you are asking.

  • Subpart A, Acronyms and Definitions. Terms like "recipient," "subrecipient," "contractor," "period of performance," and "equipment" are defined here, and the definitions decide which rules apply. The subrecipient-versus-contractor distinction alone drives an entire monitoring regime.
  • Subpart B, General Provisions. Scope, applicability, and the agency's authority to grant exceptions.
  • Subpart C, Pre-Federal Award Requirements. What agencies must do before they make an award: the funding opportunity notice, merit review, risk assessment of applicants, and the information the award itself must contain.
  • Subpart D, Post-Federal Award Requirements. The operating manual. Financial management systems, internal controls, payment and cash management, cost sharing, program income, property standards, procurement standards, performance and financial reporting, subrecipient monitoring, record retention, remedies for noncompliance, and closeout.
  • Subpart E, Cost Principles. Whether a cost may be charged to the award, and under which conditions. The general tests come first, followed by an alphabetical list of selected items of cost, from advertising to travel.
  • Subpart F, Audit Requirements. The Single Audit: who must have one, what it covers, who is responsible for what, and how findings are reported and resolved.

Subpart D: the administrative requirements that run your award

Financial management and internal controls

Section 200.302 requires a financial management system that can identify every federal award, trace spending to the approved budget, compare actual outlays to budget, and support every charge with source documentation. Section 200.303 requires effective internal control over the award, and points to the COSO framework and the Government Accountability Office's Green Book as the models. In practice, this means segregation of duties, written procedures, a reconciliation routine, and someone who reviews what someone else prepared.

Payment and cash management

When you draw federal funds in advance, the money must be spent promptly. Advance payments are limited to immediate cash needs, and interest earned on advances above the amount in the current regulation must be remitted. Reimbursement is the default when a recipient cannot meet the advance-payment standards.

Procurement

Sections 200.317 through 200.327 set the procurement standards. A nonprofit must have written procurement procedures, maintain a written conflict-of-interest standard, document the method used for each purchase, and choose that method based on the dollar value against the micro-purchase and simplified acquisition thresholds in effect. Above those thresholds, competition is expected and sole-source awards need a written justification. The required contract provisions in Appendix II must be flowed into contracts paid with federal funds.

Subrecipient monitoring

If you pass federal money to another entity to carry out part of the program, that entity is probably a subrecipient, and sections 200.331 through 200.333 make you responsible for it. You must make the subrecipient-versus-contractor determination in writing, assess the subrecipient's risk, include the required award information in the subaward, monitor performance and spending, review the subrecipient's audit results, and follow up on findings. A pass-through nonprofit that treats its subrecipients as vendors is one of the most common findings in the sector.

Reporting, record retention, and closeout

Financial reports, usually the SF-425, and performance reports are due on the schedule in the award. Records must be retained for the period in section 200.334, generally three years from submission of the final expenditure report, longer if an audit or litigation is open. Closeout under section 200.344 requires the final reports and any cash reconciliation within the closeout window stated in the regulation. Missing that window can mean the agency closes the award on its own terms.

Subpart E: the five tests every cost must pass

Section 200.403 lists the factors that make a cost allowable. The most useful way to hold them in mind is as five questions you ask about any charge before it reaches the award.

  1. Is it allowable? Permitted under the cost principles and not prohibited by the award. Section 200.420 onward lists selected items of cost, and some, such as alcohol, lobbying, and most entertainment, are expressly unallowable.
  2. Is it allocable? Section 200.405: the cost benefits the award in proportion to what is charged. A shared cost must be split by a reasonable, documented method.
  3. Is it reasonable? Section 200.404: a prudent person would pay it, in these circumstances, at this price.
  4. Is it consistently treated? A cost treated as direct on one award cannot be treated as indirect on another in like circumstances.
  5. Is it documented? Adequately supported by records that show what was bought, why, and how it was allocated.

Two items deserve their own attention. Compensation for personal services, section 200.430, requires records that reasonably reflect the work performed and that support the distribution of salaries among awards. The old mandatory time-and-effort certifications are gone, but the standard of support is not. And indirect costs, section 200.414, may be charged using a negotiated rate or, for organizations that have never had one, the de minimis rate stated in the current regulation. The de minimis percentage changed in the 2024 revision, so confirm it before you budget.

Subpart F: the Single Audit

A nonprofit that expends federal awards above the current threshold in section 200.501 in its fiscal year must have a Single Audit, or in limited cases a program-specific audit. The auditor tests both the financial statements and compliance with the requirements that apply to your major federal programs, using the annual Compliance Supplement as the guide. You prepare the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards, respond to findings with a corrective action plan, and submit the reporting package to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse within the deadline in section 200.512.

Every finding becomes public, and every pass-through entity that funds you is required to review it. That is why audit readiness is a year-round discipline rather than a spring project.

Where nonprofits most often stumble

  • No written policies, or policies written to a template. A procurement policy copied from another organization that does not match how you actually buy things is a finding waiting to happen.
  • Cost allocation by habit. Rent and shared staff split by last year's percentages, with no documented basis.
  • Subrecipients treated as vendors. No risk assessment, no monitoring, no review of their audits.
  • Budget drift without prior approval. Reallocations and scope changes that needed the agency's written approval under section 200.308 and did not get it.
  • Thin personnel documentation. Salaries charged at budgeted percentages rather than actual effort.
  • Late closeout. Final reports filed after the window, or not at all.

Building a system that complies

The organizations that stay in good standing share a few habits. They keep one calendar of every report due across every award. They have a written policy for each area the regulation names, and the policies describe what the organization actually does. They reconcile grant ledgers to the general ledger monthly. They decide allowability before a cost is incurred, not after. And they treat the Single Audit as a confirmation of systems that already work, rather than as the moment they find out whether they do.

FAQ

Common questions

Does 2 CFR 200 apply to every federal grant a nonprofit receives?+
Nearly every one. Federal agencies incorporate the Uniform Guidance into their award terms, usually alongside the agency's own adopting regulation in Title 2. Read the award terms to confirm, and check whether the agency has approved any deviations.
Does it apply to subawards we receive from a state or another nonprofit?+
Yes. A pass-through entity must flow the federal requirements down to its subrecipients, so a subaward of federal money carries the Uniform Guidance with it even though your agreement is with a state agency or a larger nonprofit.
Where do I find the current Single Audit threshold?+
In section 200.501 of the current regulation at eCFR. The threshold changed in the 2024 revision, so a figure from an older training or an older award may be wrong.
