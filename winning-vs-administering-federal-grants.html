Winning a federal grant vs. successfully administering one

Winning a federal grant obligates the funder. Administering it is what obligates you. From the award date, the recipient owes the agency a compliant financial management system, allowable costs, timely reports, monitored subrecipients, documented procurement, and a clean closeout. An organization that invests in winning and improvises administration will, sooner or later, return money, lose eligibility, or carry audit findings into its next application. The two skills are different, and the second is the one that protects the first.
Matthew F. Johnston, Founder & Principal, MFJ Grant Strategies · Published November 7, 2026
Key takeaways
  • The award letter changes your legal position. You now owe a set of specific duties under 2 CFR 200 Subpart D and the award terms.
  • Costs can be disallowed years after they were spent. Documentation at the time is the only defense.
  • Reporting on time, every time, is the single most visible signal of a competent recipient.
  • Prior approval requirements are traps for the unprepared. Budget shifts, scope changes, and key personnel changes often need written agency consent.
  • Audit findings follow you. Agencies assess applicant risk using your history, so administration quality decides future wins.

Two different skills

Winning a competitive award is a persuasion problem: fit, positioning, evidence, and writing to a rubric. Administering an award is a systems problem: controls, documentation, calendars, and compliance with rules that do not care how good the proposal was. Organizations tend to be built for one or the other. Development-led organizations win and then stumble. Finance-led organizations administer well and rarely compete. The ones that sustain federal funding over time have both, usually because someone has made administration a function rather than an afterthought.

What the award letter actually does

The notice of award is a legal instrument. It incorporates 2 CFR 200, the agency's adopting regulation, the program statute and regulations, and the award's specific terms and conditions. From that moment the recipient owes the government a list of things, and the list is longer than most new recipients expect:

  • A compliant financial management system under 200.302, able to trace every dollar to the award and the approved budget.
  • Internal controls under 200.303 that reasonably assure compliance.
  • Allowable, allocable, reasonable, documented costs under Subpart E, decided before the money is spent.
  • Cash management that limits advances to immediate need.
  • Procurement under written procedures that meet 200.317 through 200.327, with the method documented each time.
  • Subrecipient monitoring if any money is passed through.
  • Financial and performance reports on the schedule in the award.
  • Prior approval under 200.308 for budget revisions above the stated flexibility, changes in scope or objectives, changes in key personnel, and certain other actions.
  • Property and equipment records for anything purchased with award funds.
  • Record retention for the period in 200.334.
  • Closeout within the window in 200.344, with final reports and reconciliation.

Where winning organizations fail at administering

They spend first and ask later. Allowability is a decision made before a cost is incurred. Organizations that charge costs and sort them out at reporting time discover unallowable costs after the money is gone.

They move money without permission. Budget categories are not suggestions. Shifting funds beyond the award's flexibility, or changing what the project does, without written agency approval is a common finding and a common disallowance.

They treat reports as paperwork. Late or inaccurate SF-425s and performance reports are the most visible failure an agency sees. Program officers remember, and agency risk assessments under 200.206 draw on history of performance.

They lose the paper. Staff turn over, files live in personal drives, and three years later an auditor asks for a procurement file that no one can find.

They forget closeout. The period of performance ends, the program staff move on, and the final reports slide. The agency closes the award unilaterally, sometimes with an open cash balance that becomes a debt.

Why administration decides the next award

Agencies are required to consider applicant risk before awarding. Single Audit findings are public. Pass-through entities review your audit before subawarding to you. Program officers talk to one another. An organization with late reports, repeat findings, and a disallowance in its history is competing with a handicap that no proposal can write around. Conversely, a clean administrative record is an asset the organization carries into every future competition, and reviewers scoring organizational capacity can see it.

Building the second skill

The fix is organizational rather than heroic. Someone owns post-award, with the authority to say no to an unallowable cost. There is one calendar of every reporting deadline across every award. There are written policies that match practice. Monthly reconciliation of grant ledgers to the general ledger. A prior-approval log. A records system that does not depend on any one person. And a closeout checklist that starts ninety days before the period of performance ends, not after.

FAQ

Common questions

Can a cost really be disallowed years later?+
Yes. Audits, agency reviews, and Inspector General work can examine costs throughout the record-retention period and beyond if an audit or litigation is open. The only protection is documentation made at the time the cost was incurred.
What is the most common post-award mistake?+
Budget and scope changes made without the agency's written prior approval. Recipients assume the money is theirs to redirect. It is not, beyond the flexibility the award and 2 CFR 200.308 allow.
Can administration costs be charged to the award?+
Generally yes, through a negotiated indirect cost rate or the de minimis rate in the current 2 CFR 200.414, and in some cases as direct costs where the award allows. Recovering those costs is what funds the capacity to administer the award well.
